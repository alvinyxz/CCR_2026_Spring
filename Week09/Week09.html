Reply from Alvin Zhou For your first question, the limitation is exactly what you are pointing to: the model is calibrated in a small, highly controlled system. This design is powerful for identifying a clean mechanism (e.g., tipping points around ~25%), but it abstracts away many real-world factors, such as identity, ideology, power asymmetries, and heterogeneous network structures.

As a result, the model has strong (I would even say "extreme") internal validity but (very) limited external validity — they are basically using humans as lab mice and claiming that they are discovering "intrinsic human behavior patterns as basic animals." This kind of design is actually closer to what you see in parts of physics-inspired social science (an area called "complex systems", a big subfield in physics): the goal is not realism, but to identify whether a fundamental dynamic exists at all. The findings are best interpreted as evidence of a baseline coordination mechanism, not a directly generalizable prediction about real social systems.

For your second question on Network Agenda Setting, Partisan Selective Exposure, and Opinion Repertoire, you are correct that self-reported media exposure introduces measurement error. That said, this is a very standard limitation in survey-based media research. The practical consequence is some noise in the independent variable, which typically attenuates effects. In other words, if anything, the estimates are likely conservative. There are better alternatives (e.g., passive tracking, behavioral logs), but they are often infeasible or out of scope for the study.

Reply from Alvin Zhou That’s a very sharp observation lol. I was actually thinking about the same issue: whether this should be considered a “network study.” In a strict sense, this is not a study using network analysis (i.e., nodes, edges, and formal network measures). But in a broader sense, it is a network study, because it manipulates social relational structures—support vs. competition—and examines how those structures shape behavior. So I would frame it as "a study about networks", but not "a study using network analysis".

To your question about whether network analysis can be incorporated into non-controlled designs: yes, absolutely. What Zhang et al. did is one approach—embedding social structure into an RCT. But you could also imagine (1) adding network measurement into experiments (e.g., mapping who interacts with whom and then analyzing diffusion or influence patterns), or (2) using observational designs, where you collect real-world network data (e.g., interviewing people in gym about who are their friends, what's their ties with them, is it competitive or supportive, and then run a regression analysis based on the interview/survey self-report data). The trade-off is the usual one: experiments give you stronger causal identification, while observational network studies give you a more realistic structure but weaker causal claims.

Regarding your second question, I think you are right to raise concerns about construct validity. Attendance is clearly not a perfect measure of physical activity. However, in this study, attendance is used as a behavioral proxy, and in many ways, it is a strong one. Compared with self-reported physical activity (such as the CDC 3-item baseline measure), attendance is objectively recorded and directly tied to the intervention. So while it does not fully capture “exercise” as a construct, it does capture engagement with exercise opportunities, which is arguably the behavioral outcome the intervention is trying to influence.

Also, the baseline CDC measure is primarily used for screening and sample characterization, not as the main dependent variable. The authors likely want participants who are at least somewhat able to exercise; otherwise, no one would show up, and the experiment would yield little variation. In that sense, attendance becomes a more sensitive, behaviorally grounded outcome in this specific experimental context.

So overall, I would say: yes, there is some limitation in construct validity, but using attendance as the DV is a reasonable and defensible trade-off given the goal of measuring actual behavior rather than self-reports.

AuthorTeacher Mar 28 12:31am

Reply from Alvin Zhou For your first question on Centola, your intuition is correct. This type of experiment is deliberately highly simplified, and that is actually the point. The goal is not to simulate a realistic social environment, but to strip away as much context as possible and isolate a very basic social mechanism. In that sense, it is closer to a “minimal system” design, similar to approaches more common in physics or complex systems (this is a real field in physics that has spawned many computational social scientists), where researchers seek to understand fundamental dynamics under highly simplified and controlled conditions.

This is not the dominant approach in communication or traditional social science, where external validity and realism are often emphasized. But there is a line of work that prioritizes internal validity and mechanism identification over realism. The idea is that if a pattern holds even in an extremely simplified environment, it may reveal something fundamental about human behavior (e.g., coordination, diffusion, convergence). Basically, how humans, as basic animals, work in groups. That said, your reaction is reasonable; many scholars, myself included, also find these designs somewhat removed from real-world social processes.

Regarding your second question about Zhang et al. (2016), the four-arm design does not aim to exhaust all possible combinations of features (it is not like A/B testing for all features on websites). Instead, it is structured around "conceptual" conditions rather than fully factorial combinations. Each condition represents a broader intervention type (e.g., support-based vs. comparison-based environments), and each of these is implemented through a "bundle" of design features (interface, interaction structure, reward mechanism, etc.).

So rather than thinking of this as a clean 2×2 factorial design with independent dimensions, it is better understood as a comparison of distinct behavioral environments, each defined by multiple aligned cues. That is why you do not see all possible combinations (e.g., mixing support + comparison + both reward types). Including every combination would make the design more complex but not necessarily more interpretable, because the conditions would lose conceptual clarity.

Regarding manipulation checks, they are more common in survey experiments where there is uncertainty about whether participants actually perceive the manipulation or even pay attention. In contrast, in behavioral or platform-based experiments like this, the manipulation is embedded directly in the system (e.g., what users can see, how they interact, how rewards are assigned). Because exposure is strictly enforced, researchers are typically super confident that the manipulation “worked” and was applied to the participants, and formal manipulation checks are less common.

For your second question about the ICA paper, the key is to shift how we interpret the results. TERGM is not just showing that patterns like triadic closure or size differences exist; it shows that these patterns occur more often than would be expected by chance, after accounting for other structural dependencies in the network. A simpler way to interpret the results is:

A positive triadic closure effect means: if two divisions are both connected to a third division, they are more likely to also be connected to each other. In substantive terms, the network tends to “close triangles,” becoming more cohesive over time.

A size-related effect means: certain types of divisions (e.g., large vs. small) are systematically more or less likely to share members.

So the main takeaway is not just “these patterns are not random,” but rather that these are the underlying mechanisms shaping how the network evolves. TERGM allows you to move from description (“we observe clustering”) to explanation (“the network tends to form through closure and size-based tendencies”). The same can be said about ERGM.

Reply from Alvin Zhou For Wang et al., I think it is reasonable to interpret the interaction term as capturing a moderation-like effect (and I apologize for not fully understanding your question during class), but I would be cautious about calling it a “moderator” in the strict sense. The RQ4 model (y ~ time + network position + time × network position) is essentially testing whether the effect of one variable depends on the level of another. In that sense, it has the same statistical form as moderation. However, the paper does not explicitly theorize it that way. A more precise interpretation is that the interaction term captures time-varying effects, meaning that the relationship between activists’ positions in the smaller network and their centrality in the broader network changes over time. So rather than framing it as a formal moderator, it is safer to say that the model allows for conditional and non-constant effects over time. And intuitively, it makes sense that the effect of activists’ positions in the smaller network on their positions in the larger network would evolve over time. It would be ideal if the authors provided stronger theoretical justification in the literature review, but in practice, this omission feels relatively minor.

For Xu (2024), what you noticed about “statistics instead of variables” is actually a defining feature of the method rather than a choice. In TERGM/ERGM models, predictors are often network statistics that represent structural configurations, such as triangles (triadic closure) or gwdegree (degree distribution and concentration). These are included because network data violate the independence assumption of standard regression (i.e., ties are inherently interdependent). As a result, the model must explicitly account for these dependencies by incorporating structural terms. This connects to what we discussed in class: standard models like logistic regression are not appropriate for network data because they assume independence among observations. A useful way to think about it is that "including these network statistics" is exactly how we can account for that interdependence.

This added complexity also changes how we interpret the model. Instead of asking whether an independent variable predicts an outcome, the model evaluates whether certain structural patterns (e.g., closed triads) are more likely to appear in the observed network than would be expected by chance (typically assessed by simulating many possible networks, as we mentioned in class, cue "MCMC"). In other words, the model explains how the network is formed, rather than simply estimating relationships between variables.

Different studies operationalize them differently depending on what aspect of the network they want to capture. There is a "brokerage score" score in the sna R package corresponding directly to Burt's work for example.

Reply from Alvin Zhou For your first question, the experimental setup is intentionally simplified, and that is by design. The goal is not to simulate real-world political beliefs, but to isolate a basic coordination mechanism under highly controlled conditions. In that sense, it is a “minimal system” experiment: researchers strip away social context and treat participants almost like agents (or "basic animals") in a coordination game to identify fundamental dynamics (e.g., how norms emerge and shift), and to say "how humans intrinsically behave."

The idea is that if a mechanism (like tipping points in norm change) holds even in a simplified environment, it may reflect something fundamental about human behavior.
